I own and manage rentals myself, and I use the same process for every deal, whether it's for me or a client. Here's how it works.

## Step 1: Start with real rent, not hopeful rent

I pull **leased** comps (not just active listings) for similar homes nearby: same bed/bath count, similar size, similar condition. Asking rents are what landlords want. Leased rents are what tenants actually pay.

## Step 2: Use Houston-specific expenses

National rules of thumb don't work here. I always check:

- **Property taxes**: look up the actual total rate, including MUD/PID. Budget on what taxes will likely be *after* the sale, since values often get reassessed.
- **Insurance**: get a real quote. Wind, hail, and flood exposure vary a lot by location.
- **HOA**: dues plus any rental restrictions in the deed restrictions.
- **Vacancy**: I usually budget 5–8% of rent.
- **Repairs & maintenance**: 5–10% of rent depending on age and condition, plus a separate reserve for big-ticket items (roof, HVAC, water heater).
- **Management**: 8–10% if you won't self-manage.

## Step 3: Run the three numbers

1. **Monthly cash flow** = rent − all expenses − mortgage payment
2. **Cap rate** = annual net operating income ÷ purchase price
3. **Cash-on-cash return** = annual cash flow ÷ total cash invested (down payment + closing + rehab)

You can plug in your own numbers with the [rental cash-flow calculator](/invest#calculator).

## Step 4: Walk it like a rehab

I walk every property thinking about the next 5 years: roof age, HVAC age, foundation, plumbing type, drainage, and what tenants will be hard on. A "great deal" with a 20-year-old roof and original AC might not be one.

## Step 5: Stress-test it

What happens if rent is 5% lower, the house sits vacant for two months, or taxes go up? If the deal only works when everything goes perfectly, I pass.
