CH 13 Endocrine System

Everything here reduces to three questions: what kind of signal is it, what receptor does it hit, and which way does the feedback loop run?

12.5%Left after 3 half-lives (20 min half-life, 1 hour)
cAMPSecond messenger made from ATP by adenylate cyclase
I⁻Iodine — the element only the thyroid traps from blood
Ca²⁺The ion PTH raises and calcitonin lowers

Four kinds of chemical signal

Self
Autocrine — affects the very cell that secreted it.
Near
Paracrine — affects only nearby cells through interstitial fluid (sonic hedgehog, prostaglandins).
Far
Hormone — into the bloodstream, acts on distant target cells bearing its receptor.
Outside
Pheromone — released outside the body, affects another member of the same species.

Exocrine glands use ducts to a surface or cavity (sweat, salivary). Endocrine = "internal secretion," ductless, straight into interstitial fluid then blood.

Two hormone classes, two mechanisms

Steroid & steroid-like (lipid soluble: cortisol, aldosterone, estradiol, testosterone, thyroid hormones):

diffuses through the membrane → binds an intracellular receptor → complex binds DNA → directly activates gene transcription → new protein

Nonsteroid (water soluble: amines, peptides, proteins, glycoproteins):

binds a surface receptor → G protein → adenylate cyclase → ATP becomes cAMP (second messenger) → protein kinases → cascade of existing enzymes

Receptor regulation & feedback

  • Up-regulation = MORE receptors, caused by a prolonged drop in hormone level.
  • Down-regulation = FEWER receptors, caused by a prolonged rise in hormone level.
  • Negative feedback controls hormone concentration — the response shuts off the stimulus.
  • Positive feedback is the exception: oxytocin in labor. Pressure on the cervix → more oxytocin → stronger contractions → more pressure.
  • Half-life = time to clear half the molecules. Long half-life = long-lasting effect.
  • Tropic hormone = stimulates another endocrine gland to secrete (TSH, ACTH, FSH, LH).

Hypothalamus → pituitary wiring

The hypothalamus (never the thalamus) controls the pituitary two different ways:

ANTERIOR: releasing hormones travel the hypophyseal portal veins; target cells are in the anterior lobe POSTERIOR: neurosecretory cells in the hypothalamus make ADH & oxytocin; axons carry them down to be stored and released

So posterior-lobe hormones are synthesized in the hypothalamus. The intermediate lobe (pars intermedia) makes MSH.

Diabetes mellitus, exam version. Untreated type 1: cells can't take up glucose, so fat is burned — fatty acids and ketone bodies accumulate in the blood. Glucose spills into urine, so the urine is of high osmotic pressure, pulling water with it (polyuria, then polydipsia).

Stress & aging

  • The stimulus is the thing that frightens you; stress is the condition it produces.
  • General stress response: epinephrine AND cortisol both rise, blood pressure increases.
  • A stressed person has lowered resistance to infection.
  • With age: GH decreases, ADH increases. hGH supplements decrease fat and increase muscle mass but do not improve strength.

Nervous vs. endocrine — don't flip them

NERVOUS: releases neurotransmitters into synapses → fast, brief ENDOCRINE: releases hormones into the bloodstream → slower, longer lasting

All hormones are organic compounds. Nonsteroid hormones include peptides, proteins, glycoproteins and amines.

CH 14 Blood

Blood is a connective tissue. Most of this chapter is counts, colors and the clotting cascade — memorize the numbers cold, because they're free points.

45%Hematocrit — percent red blood cells
5,000–10,000Normal WBC per µL. Below 5,000 = leukopenia
130k–360kPlatelets per µL. Low = thrombocytopenia
~5 LAdult blood volume; varies with body fat content

What's in the tube

PLASMA ~55% — 92% water + proteins, gases, nutrients, electrolytes, NPNs, hormones FORMED ELEMENTS ~45% — red cells, white cells and platelets (mostly red cells)

Serum = plasma minus fibrinogen and the other clotting factors. Plasma contains fibrinogen; serum does not.

Red blood cells

  • Biconcave disc — thin near the center, thick around the rim, no nucleus when mature.
  • Made in red bone marrow; controlled by EPO from the kidneys when oxygen runs low.
  • Line: hematopoietic stem cell → erythroblast → reticulocyte → erythrocyte.
  • Needs iron (for hemoglobin) plus vitamin B₁₂ and folic acid for DNA synthesis — B₁₂ is absorbed only with intrinsic factor from the stomach.
  • Tannic acid in tea binds iron and blocks absorption of iron supplements.

Hemoglobin breakdown — follow the colors

old RBC → macrophages in liver & spleen split hemoglobin heme → iron (recycled) + biliverdin (GREENISH) biliverdin → bilirubin (ORANGE) → bile → intestine → brown feces

Hemoglobin contains iron. Cyanosis = too much deoxyhemoglobin.

Plasma proteins & NPNs

  • Albumin — most abundant; its key job is maintaining colloid osmotic pressure.
  • Alpha & beta globulins — transport lipids and fat-soluble vitamins. Gamma globulins = antibodies.
  • Fibrinogen — clotting.
  • Starvation or a protein-poor diet → blood protein falls → water leaves the blood → edema, tissues swell.
  • NPNs: urea, uric acid, creatine, creatinine, amino acids. High urea + no urination → the kidneys are failing.
  • Electrolytes: sodium, potassium, calcium, magnesium, chloride, bicarbonate, phosphate, sulfate.

Myeloblasts give rise to the granulocytes; monoblasts → monocytes; lymphoblasts → lymphocytes. Diapedesis = white cells squeezing between endothelial cells to leave the circulation, then following chemicals by positive chemotaxis. Pus = bacteria + white blood cells + damaged cells. A differential WBC count reports the percentage of each type.

Hemostasis — three steps, in order

1. Blood vessel spasm — triggered by serotonin released from platelets 2. Platelet plug — platelets stick to collagen and to each other 3. Coagulation — the fibrin clot

Platelets are cytoplasmic fragments of megakaryocytes — no nucleus.

The clotting cascade

EXTRINSIC — damaged tissue releases tissue thromboplastin; fast; measured by prothrombin time (PT) INTRINSIC — blood contacts collagen/foreign surface (factor XII); hemophilia A lacks factor VIII, stalling the factor IX step ↓ both converge prothrombin activator + Ca²⁺ prothrombin → thrombin fibrinogen → fibrin — the basic event of clot formation

Vitamin K is needed by the liver to make prothrombin.

Preventing and dissolving clots

  • Prostacyclin (PGI₂) from healthy endothelium keeps platelets from sticking to the vessel wall.
  • Heparin blocks formation of prothrombin activator and thrombin's action on fibrinogen.
  • Plasminogen → plasmin digests fibrin — no plasminogen means clots never dissolve.
  • Clot-busting drugs: urokinase and streptokinase.
  • Thrombus = clot formed abnormally in place. Embolus = clot that broke loose and travels. A pulmonary embolism is a clot reaching the lung through a pulmonary artery.
  • DVT: avoid long stretches of holding still — move on long flights.
  • Thrombocytopenia → easy bleeding and petechiae, small red bruise-like spots.

Anemias & other disorders

Iron def.
Lack of dietary iron — the most common anemia.
Pernicious
No intrinsic factor, so B₁₂ isn't absorbed.
Aplastic
Damaged red bone marrow.
Sickle cell
Caused by a mutation; hydroxyurea treats it by switching fetal hemoglobin back on.
Leukemia
Cancer of blood-forming tissue — too many abnormal white cells crowd out everything else, so you see fatigue, easy bruising, repeated infections, headaches, nosebleeds.
Leukopenia
Abnormally low WBC count (HIV, aplastic anemia).

Rh and the second pregnancy. Rh⁺ means the D antigen is present; about 15% of Caucasians and 5% of African Americans are Rh⁻. An Rh⁻ woman is sensitized by her first Rh⁺ fetus and makes anti-Rh antibodies, so erythroblastosis fetalis threatens the second Rh⁺ fetus. CH 15 Cardiovascular System

Two circuits, one four-chamber pump, one conduction pathway, and a long list of named vessels. If you can trace a drop of blood and read an ECG strip, you have most of the chapter.

7,000 LPumped per day; ~2.5 billion beats in a lifetime
CO = SV × HRCardiac output — never stroke volume × blood pressure
145 / 140 / 70Heart rate: fetus / newborn / adult
5th ICSWhere the apex points; apical beat 7.5 cm left of midline

Two circuits

PULMONARY (right side): sends oxygen-poor blood to the lungs SYSTEMIC (left side): sends oxygen-rich blood to the tissues

Circulation matters because it supplies oxygen and nutrients to tissues and carries wastes away. The aorta is the largest artery of the systemic circuit — not the pulmonary circuit.

Wall layers, inside → out

endocardium — inner lining of chambers and valves myocardium — cardiac muscle, does the pumping epicardium (= visceral pericardium) — covers the heart surface pericardial cavity → parietal pericardium → fibrous pericardium (the loose-fitting outer sac)

The skeleton of the heart is fibrous connective tissue encircling the atrioventricular and semilunar orifices — it anchors the valves and blocks impulses.

Trace the blood

vena cavae + coronary sinus → right atrium → tricuspid → right ventricle → pulmonary valve → pulmonary trunk (divides 5 cm up) → lungs → pulmonary veins → left atrium → mitral → left ventricle → aortic valve → aorta

When the ventricles contract, both AV valves close (first sound, "lubb") and the semilunar valves open. Tricuspid is loudest just right of the lower sternum; mitral at the apex.

Coronary circulation

  • Right and left coronary arteries are the first branches of the aorta; smaller branches have anastomoses.
  • The coronary sinus drains into the RIGHT atrium, not the left.
  • Narrowing the right coronary artery starves the right side of the myocardium → ischemia.
  • Angina pectoris = pain from blockage of an artery supplying the heart; a full blockage kills tissue → myocardial infarction.
  • Mitral valve prolapse: a cusp bulges back into the left atrium during ventricular contraction.
  • ANP from the atria inhibits renin and aldosterone — it lowers blood pressure.

Conduction and the ECG

The pathway

SA node (pacemaker, right atrial wall) → AV node → AV bundle (bundle of His, interventricular septum) → bundle branches → Purkinje fibers

The AV node and the Purkinje fibers can both act as a secondary pacemaker. Once SA node cells reach threshold, an action potential is triggered.

Reading the strip

P
Atrial depolarization
QRS
Ventricular depolarization (atrial repolarization hides inside it)
T
Ventricular repolarization
PQ
Time for the impulse to travel from the SA node through the AV node

Rate control

  • Cardiac control center: medulla oblongata.
  • Vagus nerve = parasympathetic = decreases heart rate. Sympathetic accelerator nerves increase rate and force.
  • Baroreceptors in the aortic arch and carotid sinuses sense stretch → parasympathetic signals → heart rate drops.
  • Chemoreceptors live in the aortic bodies and carotid bodies — they read O₂, CO₂ and pH.
  • Conducting cells are most sensitive to potassium: excess K⁺ decreases both rate and force of contraction. Excess Ca²⁺ prolongs contraction.

Arrhythmias

Brady
Abnormally slow rate. A runner at 53 bpm has sinus bradycardia — and a very healthy heart.
Tachy
Abnormally fast rate.
Flutter
Very rapid but still coordinated.
Fib
Small areas of myocardium contracting chaotically. Ventricular fibrillation is the most life-threatening.

The capillary exchange question, every time. Fluid leaves at the arteriolar end because hydrostatic pressure is higher than osmotic pressure; fluid re-enters at the venular end because osmotic pressure is higher than hydrostatic pressure. Since net inward pressure at the venular end is less than net outward pressure at the arteriolar end, more fluid leaves than returns — lymphatic vessels pick up the difference. Plasma proteins (albumin) maintain that osmotic pressure. Histamine increases permeability → edema and pain. Lipid-soluble substances diffuse straight through the membranes; water-soluble ones use channels and slits between the cells.

Blood pressure

  • Systolic = ventricular contraction (highest arterial pressure); diastolic = ventricular relaxation. Pulse pressure = systolic − diastolic.
  • Measured with a sphygmomanometer.
  • Determined by cardiac output, blood volume, peripheral resistance and viscosity. Arterial smooth muscle contracts → BP rises.
  • Frank-Starling law: the volume discharged equals the volume that entered — more stretch, more forceful contraction.
  • Sympathetic stimulation increases stroke volume.
  • Kidney disease raises BP by activating the renin-angiotensin mechanism; angiotensin II raises peripheral resistance.
  • Nitric oxide is the gas released by endothelium — it relaxes vessel smooth muscle, which is why warming up feels like a rush to the fingers and toes.

Getting blood back to the heart

  • Contracting skeletal muscles squeeze the veins; the valves make it a one-way pump. This is why you flex your ankles on a long flight.
  • Breathing: negative intrathoracic pressure pulls blood toward the chest.
  • When venous pressure drops too low, sympathetic reflexes constrict the smooth muscle in vein walls.
  • Central venous pressure is the pressure in the right atrium.

Exercise, aging, athletes

  • Exercise: heart rate up, ventricular contraction up, flow to skeletal muscle up — and flow to the abdominal viscera DOWN.
  • Aging: the tunica interna thickens, arteries grow more rigid, resting cardiac output falls.
  • An endurance runner with an enlarged heart and low resting rate and pressure has a very healthy cardiovascular system.

Disorders

Athero
Plaque in artery walls → raised risk of a thrombus or embolus. Risks: smoking, inactivity, high blood pressure, diabetes, saturated fat. Unsaturated fat and low blood pressure are not risks.
Arrest
Sudden cardiac arrest can come from scar tissue left by an earlier heart attack.
LVAD
Maintains cardiac function until a donor heart is available.
Phlebitis
Inflammation of the veins.
VEGF
Stimulates capillary growth. Blocking it stops leaky vessels in wet macular degeneration; as an angiogenesis inhibitor it starves tumors by cutting off their blood supply.
